Prepared September 28, 2026. Use this manual alongside the College Board files in your Downloads/SAT folder. The earlier, shorter `SAT Study Guide.md` remains available as a quick roadmap.

This manual teaches methods, explains common mistakes, provides original worked examples and practice, and points you to verified questions in your own bundles. It is not a question-by-question solution book for all eight tests. The question references were checked against representative questions and explanations in the local paper-test PDFs. All mini-lessons and practice questions written out here are original teaching material unless explicitly identified otherwise.

You do not need to read the entire manual in one sitting. Read one lesson, work through its examples with a pencil, and then solve questions without looking at the explanation. Return to the lesson when your mistakes show that you need it.

## 2. The study process that makes practice useful

Solving questions tells you what you can currently do. Reviewing them well changes what you can do next time. Treat these as separate jobs and make time for both.

### The five-step learning cycle

**Learn:** Read one concept. Explain it in ordinary language. If you cannot explain what a slope represents, memorizing the slope formula is not enough.

**Attempt:** Solve a small set independently. Do not check the answer after each line of work. Give yourself room to think and make a complete attempt.

**Diagnose:** Identify the specific cause of each error. “Bad at reading” is too broad. “Chose an answer that turned a possibility into a certainty” tells you what to change.

**Repair:** Study the explanation, then close it and solve from scratch. Merely following an explanation with your eyes is easier than producing the reasoning yourself.

**Retest:** Return later and solve a fresh question testing the same skill. Remembering an answer from yesterday does not show that you can transfer the method.

### Three types of correct answers

Classify your work as confident-correct, uncertain-correct, or incorrect. Correct guesses belong in your review pile. You want a method that works repeatedly, not just an answer that happened to match the key.

### Start with evidence about yourself

Use Test 4 as a baseline, unless you have already practiced it enough to remember its answers. Complete its modules independently, across multiple sessions if necessary. Record elapsed time without squeezing paper modules into digital limits. After scoring, list two Reading and Writing weaknesses and two Math weaknesses.

Prioritize skills that you miss repeatedly and can improve through a clear rule or method. Sentence boundaries, percentages, linear equations, and identifying the exact claim in a reading passage are useful starting points when your diagnostic reveals gaps there. Your personal results take priority over this suggested order.

## 3. Reading: vocabulary and precision

### Predict the meaning before reading the options

A vocabulary question tests what a word means in this particular sentence. A familiar definition may be the wrong definition for the context. Read the entire passage, notice the relationship between its ideas, and invent a simple replacement for the blank.

Original example: “The new measurements did not support the team's prediction; instead, they _____ it.”

Before seeing choices, predict “went against” or “challenged.” The phrase “did not support” and the word “instead” establish opposition. A choice such as “contradicted” would fit; “confirmed” would reverse the passage's meaning.

### Watch for logical signals

| Signal | What to investigate |
|---|---|
| although, despite, however | A contrast or unexpected result |
| because, therefore, consequently | A reason or consequence |
| indeed, in fact | Reinforcement or emphasis |
| for example, such as | An illustration of an earlier idea |
| not, rarely, hardly, by no means | Negation or limitation |

Do not treat this as mechanical word matching. “Although” tells you a contrast exists, but you still must identify which two ideas contrast.

### Separate tone from exact meaning

Suppose a passage says a researcher changed an old method slightly. “Innovative,” “revolutionary,” and “modified” may all sound related to change, but only the last directly captures a small alteration. Stronger language is not automatically better language.

### Learn vocabulary with usable distinctions

| Word | Useful meaning | Contrast worth remembering |
|---|---|---|
| corroborate | Support with additional evidence | Contradict: conflict with |
| substantiate | Provide evidence for | Assert: state, without necessarily proving |
| mitigate | Reduce severity | Eliminate: remove entirely |
| exacerbate | Make worse | Alleviate: ease |
| ambiguous | Open to multiple interpretations | Unambiguous: clear in meaning |
| tentative | Provisional or uncertain | Definitive: conclusive |
| plausible | Reasonably believable | Proven: established by sufficient evidence |
| pervasive | Widespread | Isolated: limited or separate |
| pragmatic | Focused on practical results | Impractical: difficult to apply effectively |
| conventional | Following established practice | Novel: new or unusual |
| coherent | Logically connected | Incoherent: lacking a clear connection |
| redundant | Unnecessarily repetitive | Essential: necessary |
| reconcile | Make apparently conflicting things compatible | Refute: show a claim is wrong |
| concede | Acknowledge a point, often reluctantly | Insist: maintain a position firmly |
| qualify | Limit or modify a claim | Endorse: support |
| empirical | Based on observation or experiment | Speculative: based mainly on conjecture |
| negligible | Small enough to be of little importance | Substantial: considerable |
| explicit | Directly stated | Implicit: suggested rather than stated |

These meanings are study aids, not exhaustive dictionary entries. For each new word, write your own sentence and explain why a nearby alternative would not fit.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 4, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Questions 1–4. The early questions offer useful practice with contextual evidence and negation.

## 4. Reading: central ideas and details

### Distinguish subject from main idea

A subject is a topic: “urban gardens.” A main idea makes a claim about that topic: “Urban gardens can improve residents' access to fresh food.” If your summary is only a noun phrase, you probably have the topic rather than the author's main point.

After reading, complete: “The author mainly explains that…” Aim for one sentence that covers the passage without adding outside information.

### Worked example

Original passage: “Researchers expected older trees to grow more slowly than younger trees. In their sample, however, several older trees added more wood each year than many younger trees. The researchers caution that the pattern varied by species.”

A sound main idea is: “Some older trees in the sample grew more than expected, although the pattern depended on species.”

Three tempting but wrong summaries:

- “Old trees always grow faster.” This turns a limited finding into a universal rule.
- “The researchers studied trees.” This names the subject but misses the finding.
- “Planting older trees is the best way to restore forests.” This introduces a recommendation the passage never makes.

### Detail questions require locating, not inventing

If a question asks why an event occurred, find the stated reason. Do not substitute a reasonable real-world explanation. If the text says a shop closed because its lease expired, an answer about falling sales is unsupported unless the passage says so.

### The four-part answer audit

Ask whether an answer is supported, relevant, appropriately broad, and appropriately strong. A choice can fail any one of these tests. A true detail can be too narrow for a main-idea question. A broad statement can be too strong even when its topic is correct.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 4, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 10; Test 6, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 10. Write your one-sentence summary before looking at the options.

## 5. Reading: inferences and evidence

### An inference must be anchored in the passage

An inference goes beyond repeating a sentence, but it must follow from what the passage gives you. Choose the best-supported conclusion among the options, not the most imaginative explanation.

Original example: “The archive contains letters written before 1840 and letters written after 1860, but none from the intervening years. The curator has not determined why.”

Supported conclusion: the archive has a gap in its coverage of that period.

Unsupported conclusion: all letters from that period were destroyed by fire. The gap exists, but its cause is unknown.

### Match the strength of the evidence

| Passage says | Do not silently change it to |
|---|---|
| Some participants improved | All participants improved |
| A result may indicate a cause | The cause is proven |
| Two variables are associated | One variable causes the other |
| One explanation is possible | No other explanation is possible |
| A method worked in one setting | It works in every setting |

Strong answers can be correct when the passage supports them. The rule is to match the evidence, not to reject every answer containing a strong word.

### Supporting and weakening claims

First write the claim in plain language. Then ask what observation would be more likely if the claim were true, or what observation would expose a problem with it.

Original claim: a coating reduces water loss from leaves. Helpful evidence would compare otherwise similar coated and uncoated leaves and show less water loss in the coated group. Merely showing that the coating is inexpensive does not test the claim.

Weakening evidence does not always prove the opposite. It may show that the proposed explanation is unnecessary, that a comparison was unfair, or that a prediction did not occur.

### Quote-based evidence

If the task asks for evidence that a character feels conflicted, choose a quotation containing opposing feelings. A quotation showing only enthusiasm may be true to the story but fail to establish conflict. Identify the exact trait or claim before choosing the quote.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 7, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 10 for inference; Test 5, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 15 for textual evidence.

## 6. Reading: structure, purpose, and paired texts

### Content and function are different

Content tells you what a sentence says. Function tells you what it does in the passage. A sentence describing an old theory might introduce a view that the next sentence challenges. Its function is not simply “discuss science.”

Useful function verbs include introduce, illustrate, explain, qualify, challenge, contrast, support, and conclude. Try attaching one verb to each sentence.

### Map a passage in a few words

Example structure: “Old belief → new evidence → revised conclusion.” Another is “Problem → proposed method → test result → limitation.” Once you know the structure, eliminate options that put the steps in the wrong order or assign the wrong role to the evidence.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 5, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 10.

### Paired texts: compare claims, not just topics

Original Text 1 claim: “A rise in bird sightings shows that the local bird population increased.”

Original Text 2 observation: “More volunteers joined the survey this year, and each volunteer surveyed for longer.”

Text 2 suggests an alternative explanation for the increased sightings. It does not establish that the population decreased, nor does it necessarily deny that the population could have increased. The precise relationship is a challenge to the strength of the inference in Text 1.

For each text, record the claim, evidence, and level of certainty. Then answer the specific question about agreement, disagreement, or likely response.

**Later checkpoint practice:** Test 10, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 10. Leave it untouched until your Test 10 checkpoint if you want a fresher assessment.

## 7. Reading: scientific passages, graphs, and tables

### Translate technical language into roles

You can label an unfamiliar organism “species A” and an unfamiliar chemical “substance B” while keeping the relationships intact. Do not skip a technical term if its identity matters to the comparison, but do not let an unfamiliar name stop you from tracking the argument.

Look for four elements: research question, comparison or method, result, and interpretation. Separate observations from explanations. “The treated plants grew taller” is an observation; “the treatment changed nutrient uptake” may be an explanation requiring additional evidence.

### Read graphs in a fixed order

Read the title, horizontal axis, vertical axis, units, legend, and then the data relevant to the question. Check whether the graph shows totals, percentages, changes, or averages. A tall bar is not automatically a large percentage increase.

### Worked example: total versus change

Original data:

| Town | Visitors in year 1 | Visitors in year 2 |
|---|---:|---:|
| A | 100 | 150 |
| B | 300 | 360 |

Town B has more visitors in year 2 and a larger numerical increase: 60 versus 50. Town A has the larger percentage increase: 50/100 = 50%, compared with 60/300 = 20%. The right comparison depends on the question.

### Beware of a true but irrelevant number

An answer might quote a value accurately while comparing the wrong groups. If a claim concerns change over time within one group, comparing two groups at one time may not support it.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 4, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 15. Read the claim and table together rather than treating the graph as decoration.

## 8. Grammar: clauses and sentence boundaries

### Find the sentence's skeleton

A clause contains a subject and a verb. An independent clause can stand as a complete sentence. A dependent clause cannot stand alone in the intended sentence structure.

- Independent: “The researchers collected samples.”
- Dependent: “Because the researchers collected samples.”
- Phrase: “After collecting samples.”

The dependent clause has a subject and verb, but “because” leaves the thought unfinished. The phrase does not have its own subject-plus-finite-verb structure.

### Four dependable ways to connect independent clauses

1. Period: “The sample was small. The result was useful.”
2. Semicolon: “The sample was small; the result was useful.”
3. Comma plus coordinating conjunction: “The sample was small, but the result was useful.”
4. Colon when the second part explains or develops the first: “The result was surprising: every sample changed color.”

A comma alone does not normally join two independent clauses. “The sample was small, the result was useful” is a comma splice.

### Conjunctive adverbs are not conjunctions

“However,” “therefore,” and “nevertheless” do not perform the same grammatical job as “but,” “so,” and “and.”

Correct: “The sample was small; however, the result was useful.”

Incorrect: “The sample was small, however, the result was useful.”

The second sentence uses commas where a stronger sentence boundary is needed.

### A practical decision process

When answer choices differ mostly in punctuation, temporarily ignore their meaning and examine the structure around the blank. Find each subject and main verb. Decide whether you have two independent clauses, an independent clause plus a dependent clause, or a sentence with an inserted phrase. Then choose punctuation that fits that structure.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 6, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 25.

## 9. Grammar: punctuation, possession, agreement, and modifiers

### Commas around nonessential information

“The telescope, which was installed last year, needs repairs.” The inserted clause gives extra information. Remove it and the main sentence still works: “The telescope needs repairs.”

Compare “The telescope that was installed last year needs repairs.” Here the clause helps identify which telescope. Do not surround an essential identifying clause with commas merely because it is long.

Punctuation depends on the intended structure and meaning. The deletion test helps, but it does not mean every removable phrase is automatically nonessential in context.

### Names and titles

“Biologist Maya Chen published the findings.” The name identifies the biologist; no commas are needed in that structure.

“The project's lead biologist, Maya Chen, published the findings.” The sentence presents a uniquely identified role and adds the name as supplementary information.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 5, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 25.

### Colons and semicolons in lists

A colon normally follows a complete clause when it introduces a list: “We packed three tools: a ruler, a compass, and a calculator.” Avoid “We packed: a ruler…” because the colon separates the verb from its object.

Semicolons can separate complex list items containing commas: “The visitors came from Seoul, South Korea; Lima, Peru; and Accra, Ghana.” Here each semicolon separates one city-country item.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 4, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 25. Later use Test 10, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 25 for a colon introducing an explanation.

### Possession and contractions

- One scientist's notes: notes belonging to one scientist.
- Several scientists' notes: notes belonging to multiple scientists.
- Children's books: an irregular plural takes apostrophe-s.
- Its surface: possessive, no apostrophe.
- It's changing: contraction of “it is changing.”
- Their method: possessive.
- They're ready: “they are ready.”
- There is a problem: location or introductory “there.”

To test “it's,” replace it with “it is” or “it has.” If neither works, you probably need “its.”

### Subject–verb agreement

Find the subject, then match the verb to its number. Intervening nouns are distractions: “The collection of rare books **is** valuable.” “Collection” is singular, even though “books” is plural.

“Each of the students **has** a notebook.” “Each” is singular. “The students **have** notebooks.” The plural subject requires “have.”

Do not insert a comma between a subject and its verb just because the subject is long: “The collection of books donated by former students **fills** two shelves.”

### Verb tense and verb form

Use the timeline the sentence establishes. Present tense can express a general fact; past tense can report a completed event; past perfect can mark an event completed before another past reference point.

Original example: “By the time the exhibit opened, the artist **had completed** the final sculpture.” The completion precedes the opening.

An -ing form may describe an action without serving as the main finite verb. “The scientist analyzing the samples” is a noun phrase, not a complete sentence. “The scientist analyzing the samples **noticed** a pattern” has a main verb.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 9, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 25.

### Modifiers

An introductory modifying phrase should attach logically to the subject that follows it.

Incorrect: “Walking through the museum, the paintings impressed Leila.” The paintings were not walking.

Correct: “Walking through the museum, Leila was impressed by the paintings.”

When several answer choices rearrange the beginning of a sentence, identify who or what performs the introductory action before checking the rest.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 7, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 25.

### Parallel structure and logical comparisons

Keep items in a series grammatically comparable: “The course teaches students to analyze data, construct models, and explain results.” Avoid switching arbitrarily to “the explanation of results” in the last item.

Compare like with like. “The population of City A exceeds City B” compares a population with a city. “The population of City A exceeds **that of** City B” compares two populations.

## 10. Writing: transitions and student notes

### Name the relationship first

Before choosing a transition, write a label: contrast, result, example, addition, similarity, sequence, or concession. Then choose an expression that matches.

Original example: “The first design used fewer materials. _____, it cost more to manufacture because assembly was difficult.” The second sentence contrasts with a likely expectation from the first. “However” fits; “therefore” does not describe the actual relationship.

Do not infer cause merely because one sentence follows another. “The lab moved in June. The team published in July” establishes a sequence, not proof that the move caused publication.

### Common transition groups

| Relationship | Examples |
|---|---|
| Contrast | however, nevertheless, by contrast |
| Result | therefore, consequently, as a result |
| Example | for example, for instance |
| Addition | moreover, furthermore, additionally |
| Similarity | similarly, likewise |
| Restatement | in other words, that is |
| Sequence | subsequently, afterward, previously |

These groups overlap with context-dependent meanings. Read the actual sentences rather than using a word list as an automatic answer key.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 4, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 30.

### Student-note questions: obey the requested goal

The task may ask you to emphasize a similarity, explain a difference, introduce a person to an unfamiliar audience, or provide a specific result. Read that task before studying every note.

Original notes: Researcher A studied coastal birds. Researcher B studied mountain birds. Both used GPS tags. If the goal is to emphasize a similarity in their methods, the answer should mention GPS tags. A sentence contrasting habitats may be accurate but fail the task.

Check two things separately: factual accuracy and goal completion. You need both. An answer that includes every note can be less effective than a shorter answer that selects exactly what matters.

**Your bundle practice:** Test 4, Reading and Writing, Module 1, Question 33.
